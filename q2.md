## 1. Z-Scores

A **z-score** tells you how many standard deviations an observation is from the mean.

### Formula

$$
z = \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}
$$

Where:

- $x$ = raw observation
- $\mu$ = population mean
- $\sigma$ = population standard deviation
- $z$ = z-score

### Finding the raw value

$$
x = \mu + z\sigma
$$

### Interpretation

| z-score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $z=0$ | Exactly at the mean |
| $z>0$ | Above the mean |
| $z<0$ | Below the mean |
| Larger $|z|$ | Farther from the mean / more unusual |

### Example

Mean = 420, SD = 60, observation = 510.

$$
z = \frac{510-420}{60}
= \frac{90}{60}
= 1.5
$$

Therefore, 510 is **1.5 standard deviations above the mean**.

# 2. Normal Distribution

A Normal distribution is:

- Bell-shaped
- Approximately symmetric
- Centred around the mean
- Without a pronounced tail

For a Normal distribution:

$$
\boxed{\text{Mean}=\text{Median}=\text{Mode}}
$$

### Important feature

**Approximate symmetry** is consistent with a Normal distribution.

---

# 3. Skewness

## Positive / Right Skew

The long tail extends toward **larger values**.

$$
\boxed{\text{Tail to the right} \Rightarrow \text{Positive skew}}
$$

## Negative / Left Skew

The long tail extends toward **smaller values**.

$$
\boxed{\text{Tail to the left} \Rightarrow \text{Negative skew}}
$$

### Memory rule

> **The tail tells you the direction of the skew.**

---

# 4. Outliers and Resistant Measures

Extreme observations can strongly influence the **mean**.

The **median** is generally more resistant to extreme observations.

$$
\boxed{\text{Median is resistant to outliers}}
$$

For a strongly right-skewed distribution caused by exceptionally large observations, the median is generally more resistant than the mean.

---

# 5. Normal Probability Plot

A Normal probability plot can be used to assess whether a variable is approximately Normally distributed.

### What to look for

If the points approximately follow a **straight diagonal pattern**:

$$
\boxed{\text{Variable is approximately Normally distributed}}
$$

Small deviations, particularly at the extremes, do not necessarily mean the data are not approximately Normal.

---

# 6. Continuous Random Variables

For a continuous Normally distributed variable:

$$
\boxed{P(X=x)=0}
$$

The probability of observing **one exact specified value** is zero.

Probabilities are instead considered over intervals, such as:

$$
P(a<X<b)
$$

### Important

A probability of zero for an exact value does **not** mean that value cannot occur.

---

# 7. Normal Distribution and Percentiles

For a Normal distribution, the mean corresponds to the:

$$
\boxed{50^{th}\text{ percentile}}
$$

Some useful approximations:

| Percentile | Approx. z-score |
|---:|---:|
| 16th | $-1$ |
| 50th | $0$ |
| 84th | $+1$ |

### Example

Mean = 200  
SD = 30

Approximately the 84th percentile corresponds to:

$$
x = 200+(1)(30)
$$

$$
\boxed{x=230}
$$
